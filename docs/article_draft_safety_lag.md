# The Safety Lag: Schools Wrote AI Homework Rules. Kids Got Deepfakes.

*Draft rewrite for Pete — operator-brief spine + parent email CTA. no-ai-slop pass 2026-09-09. Data: AI Policy Map `districts.csv`, Georgia traditional districts as of 2026-09-09. Not published.*

---

If you take away one idea from this, it’s that **most school AI policies were written for cheating.** Harm got the leftover attention. That lag isn’t a paperwork problem. It’s a kid-safety problem with a delayed invoice.

## 1. The problem

We’ve spent the last few years watching districts scramble to answer one question: *Can my student use ChatGPT on the essay?* Call that Clarity. It’s real. Teachers needed it. Boards needed it. Parents asked for it.

While we argued about citations and “approved tools,” another question showed up on phones after school:

**Can a kid make a fake nude or a fake voice of a real classmate — and what happens when they do?**

Call that Safety. For most public school districts in Georgia, the public answer is still silence.

I’m not talking about a vibe. I’m talking about words parents can find and quote:

1. **Deepfake / false-image language** — creating or sharing AI-generated or altered images or videos of real people
2. **Impersonation language** — fake voice, fake face, posing as someone else
3. **A tie to bullying, harassment, or Title IX rules** — so this isn’t treated as a cute tech prank
4. **A durable source** — handbook, student code of conduct, or board policy you can click

A polished AI “position statement” about responsible use and human oversight is governance. That’s Clarity. It is not a deepfake playbook by itself.

**Where the rule lives matters less than whether it exists.** If the fake-image ban is in the student code of conduct and not the board AI policy, that still counts — as long as parents can find the link. The product mistake is flattening “they have an AI policy” and missing the Safety sentence in the handbook.

Fulton County Schools is the clean split. Their board AI policy (IFBI) requires district-approved AI tools and human decision-making. Solid Clarity. The rule that bans creating and sharing AI-generated or false images of students and staff lives in the **student code of conduct, Rule 18k.III**. Read only the board policy and you miss the Safety punch.

## 2. What we see across Georgia

We mapped publicly available AI rules for Georgia’s **180 traditional** school districts.

The scoreboard (as of 2026-09-09):

- **15** districts have any public AI-safety signal (board policy, handbook, or guidance that addresses harmful AI use)
- **7** have an explicit **deepfake / false-image** ban we can point to
- **165** still show **no public Safety rule found**

Seven out of one hundred eighty.

The seven with deepfake language, and where it lives:

| District | Instrument (public) |
|---|---|
| Atlanta Public Schools | Handbook Rule 14D |
| Fulton County Schools | Student code of conduct Rule 18k.III |
| Cobb County School District | JCDA-R §I.3 (False Information) |
| Clayton County Public Schools | Handbook — Deepfake/Deepfaking |
| Henry County Schools | Student code of conduct Rule 2.3 |
| Richmond County School System | COSCD Rule 5(B) |
| Catoosa County Schools | Handbook AUP — Digital impersonation |

Most of that list is **handbook / student code of conduct**, not a shiny board AI policy. They wrote the sentence where discipline actually lives.

Everyone else is still living on last decade’s internet rules: “don’t be inappropriate online,” “don’t cheat,” “follow the acceptable use policy.” Those rules were written for MySpace and stolen passwords. They were not written for a free app that can undress a 14-year-old’s yearbook photo in thirty seconds.

This isn’t because superintendents don’t care. Districts triage. The scoreboard that actually runs the building is **Safety, Graduation, Attendance.** Personal finance electives and “new AI policy” fight for leftover oxygen. So AI work often lands where it’s easiest to staff: academic honesty. Teachers feel that pain daily. Deepfake cases feel rarer until they’re not. Then the handbook has no playbook, discipline gets improvised, victims get mishandled, and parents learn about it from Snapchat, not from the district website.

|---|---:|---:|---:|

Metro isn’t “solved.” Two-thirds of the metro-core list still show no public Safety signal. But the deepfake sentences we *can* find cluster harder in the metro ring (APS, Fulton, Cobb, Clayton, Henry) than across the rest of the state (Richmond, Catoosa).

If you’re a parent outside that ring, the silence is thicker.

## 4. How this stacks nationally

`[NEED DATA — no 50-state map yet.]` We have a full public-rule census for Georgia traditional LEAs. We do **not** have the same mapped scoreboard for all 50 states. Don’t pretend we do.

What we can say without inventing a national percent:

- The **incidents** are already national: Westfield, New Jersey. Beverly Hills. Miami-Dade. Louisiana middle schools. Principals in a RAND survey reported deepfake bullying in a meaningful share of middle and high schools. The cases are not theoretical. The policy lag is.
- At least one state put a **floor** under this. Illinois started requiring districts to update cyberbullying rules for AI deepfakes. Georgia does not have that floor yet. So districts invent, or they don’t.

Until we map more states the same way we mapped Georgia, treat national claims as pattern + incidents + state floors — not as a fake nationwide “X% have deepfake bans” statistic.

## 5. The gap

When Safety language is missing, kids don’t get a free pass. They get **ambiguity**, and ambiguity is worse.

- **Victims** don’t know if the school has a clear rule, a clear reporting path, or a clear escalation into bullying, harassment, or Title IX processes.
- **Bystanders** don’t know whether sharing “the fake” is a joke or a suspendable offense.
- **Parents** can’t pressure what they can’t find. “We take bullying seriously” is not the same as “we ban AI-generated false images of students.”
- **Teachers and principals** get left holding a case with no written instrument. That’s how you get inconsistent discipline and lawsuits.
- **Districts with strong Clarity** can still look ahead on AI homework rules while staying soft on the harm that keeps parents up at night.

The gap has two faces:

1. **Missing** — no public fake-image / deepfake rule we can quote (165 Georgia traditional districts today).
2. **Buried** — the rule exists in a handbook or student code of conduct, but parents only got pointed at a board AI policy that never mentions fake images (Fulton IFBI vs Rule 18k.III).

For schools, location is secondary. Existence + a clickable link is the product. AI Policy Map should show the written fake-image rule whether it lives in board policy, handbook, or student code of conduct — and name which.

## 6. What resolution should look like

Not a vibe score. Not a poster about “responsible AI.” A public strip parents can check:

- Fake images / deepfakes: **yes, here’s the quote and the link** — or **no public rule found**
- Impersonation: same
- Tied to bullying / harassment rules: same

“No public rule found” should feel uncomfortable. Silence is data.

For districts, resolution looks like this:

1. Write the Safety sentences (fake images, impersonation).
2. Put them where kids and parents can find them — handbook, student code of conduct, or board policy.
3. Tie them to bullying and harassment rules.
4. Train people on the playbook **before** the incident, not after.
5. Stop treating homework Clarity as a substitute for Safety.

For Georgia, a serious floor would look more like Illinois than like hoping 7 handbooks scale to 180 on their own. Until that floor exists, local boards and local parents are the pressure system.

## 7. Parent call to action (email the board)

If you’re a parent in Georgia, don’t wait for a state bill. Look up your district on **AI Policy Map**: https://ai-policy-map.pages.dev/

Ask one question — at the next board meeting, or in an email you can send from the district page:

**“Where is our written rule on AI-generated or fake images of students — and can you show me the link?”**

If the answer is a shrug, that’s the lag.

**Email the board from your district page** (confirm the message before it sends; we don’t send without your OK). The default ask is short: put two items on a coming agenda — classroom AI rules families can find, and the written fake-image rule with a link from the handbook, student code of conduct, or board policy.

Homework rules without Safety rules leave kids improvising and parents guessing. The fix starts with a sentence you can quote and a link you can click.
